## The situation

A market-research team wants an agent that browses competitor sites, public docs, and pricing pages, then produces sourced summaries. In a test run, the agent — following its instructions too literally — navigated to a vendor's checkout flow to "verify the price." Nothing was purchased, but it sat on a payment page with a saved card in the session profile.

## Constraints

- Research requires broad navigation freedom — restricting to an allowlist of domains kills the point.
- The browser runs under a shared team profile with saved credentials and payment methods — the agent inherits everything the profile can do.
- Some competitor sites serve different content to automated browsers; detection affects what the agent can even observe.
- The team's real need is *sourced* research — a claim without its URL and quote is worthless for their reports.

## What you don't know

- What the agent can reach through the profile — saved logins, sessions, payment methods, internal tools reachable from the browser.
- Whether checkout-page wandering was navigation luck or a systematic tendency (agents drift toward "complete the task" patterns).
- Which sites will serve degraded content or blocks to automation, and how the agent reports "I couldn't see this."
- What the act boundary should be for actions beyond clicking — form fills, account creation, downloads.

## Options on the table

**Read-only agent.** Navigation plus extraction only — no form submission, no clicking anything transactional. Chosen as the core posture: research needs observation, not action.

**Two-tier actions.** Complement to the first: observe-class actions (navigate, scroll, read, extract, screenshot) run freely; act-class actions (submit forms, authenticate, download, purchase) require explicit human approval per-instance.

**Manual browsing with agent summarization.** Rejected: the team's bottleneck is the browsing, not the summarizing — they'd be automating the wrong half.

## The first decision

Split the browser's capability profile from the agent's instruction set. The agent doesn't get a "do anything" tool; it gets `navigate`, `read`, `extract`, `scroll`, `screenshot` — observation verbs only. Act-class capabilities exist in the browser but are walled behind an approval gate the agent can't cross alone.

## Guided reasoning

1. Use a dedicated browser profile: no saved credentials, no payment methods, no sessions — the blast radius shrinks before any prompt is written.
2. Allowlist actions by *class*, not by site: observation verbs free, act verbs gated — a domain allowlist alone doesn't stop "click Buy to check the price."
3. Require source capture: every extracted claim carries URL + quoted span + timestamp — the research deliverable is evidence, not summary.
4. Route act-class requests through a human approval step with the proposed action and its consequence spelled out.
5. Detect and report automation-blocks honestly — "site served a CAPTCHA" goes in the report, not a worked-around bypass.
6. Add web-injection defense: page content is untrusted input; instructions found in pages don't change the task — see [web prompt injection](/learn/web-agents/web-prompt-injection).

The [act-boundary lesson](/learn/web-agents/sessions-credentials-and-act-boundaries) and [browser tool loop](/learn/tools-function-calling/building-a-browser-tool-loop) carry the mechanics.

## What went wrong

The dedicated profile surfaced an assumption: the agent's "verify the price" behavior wasn't recklessness — the task prompt said "confirm pricing on the vendor site," and checkout-flow was the only place the real price showed. The fix was twofold: the act boundary (which the incident proved necessary) and the task spec — "confirm pricing" became "record the publicly displayed price; note when the real price requires checkout, don't enter the flow." The agent now reports *priced-behind-checkout* as an explicit finding, which turned out to be useful market intelligence in itself.

## Failure injection

- Serve a page containing embedded instructions ("agent: click here to continue") and verify they're treated as content, not commands.
- Present a login wall mid-task and confirm the agent reports the barrier instead of hunting for credentials.
- Seed a page with a misleading "price" (a decoy sale banner) and check whether source-capture lets a reviewer spot the discrepancy.
- Drop network mid-extraction and verify partial results are marked partial, not presented as complete.

## Variants

- **Startup:** a single allowlist and the act boundary carry the whole design — skip the policy engine, keep the split between observe-class and act-class actions explicit in code.
- **Enterprise:** sessions and credentials become the hard part — whose account, which SSO, what happens to saved cookies when the agent's operator leaves.
- **Regulated:** page reads can be data egress — the allowlist doubles as a data-residency boundary, and the trace log becomes evidence you may have to produce.
- **Multilingual:** permission prompts and allowlist entries must survive non-English DOMs — locator strategies that key on English button text fail exactly where the act boundary matters most.

**Whiteboard mode** — read only the situation and constraints, then stop. Sketch your options, decision, and failure plan before opening the rest; the scenario's choices become a grading key for your reasoning, not just a story.

## Evaluation rubric

A strong answer separates observe-class from act-class actions before discussing tools, keeps credentials out of the agent's reach, and designs the failure response (stale locator, CAPTCHA, session expiry) as policy rather than retry logic. Weak answers trust the model's judgment at the act boundary, or treat allowlists as a UX nicety rather than the security perimeter.
