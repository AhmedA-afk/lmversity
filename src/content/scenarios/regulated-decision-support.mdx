## The situation

A regional agency processes benefit applications. Caseworkers spend most of their time locating which of forty-plus eligibility rules apply to each application. Leadership wants AI to "speed up eligibility decisions." Legal has already flagged the phrase "AI decides eligibility" as a non-starter.

## Constraints

- Every decision must be explainable in terms of specific rules — "the model felt this applicant qualified" is not an explanation a regulator or an appeal board accepts.
- Applicants have appeal rights; the system must support "show me exactly why I was denied."
- Caseworker judgment is legally required for the actual determination; the AI can only assist.
- Application documents contain sensitive personal data with retention limits — nothing may be retained that isn't required.
- Accuracy errors are asymmetric: a wrongly-denied applicant loses benefits; a wrongly-approved one creates an overpayment recovery case that harms the applicant later anyway.

## What you don't know

- How well the forty-plus rules are actually documented — rule text, informal caseworker practice, and actual decisions may be three different standards.
- The error distribution of the current manual process — the AI's bar is "better than today," not perfect.
- Whether past decisions can be used as eval data (consent, representativeness, label quality — "decision recorded" is not "decision correct").
- Which steps in the pipeline handle the sensitive data, and where it must be redacted or excluded.

## Options on the table

**Model makes the determination, human rubber-stamps.** Rejected: legally indefensible — the formal decision-maker must actually decide, and a stamp queue decays into auto-approval.

**Extract-and-match assistant.** Chosen: the model reads the application and produces a structured extraction (facts claimed, documents present, rules potentially applicable with citations to rule text). The caseworker sees a prepared worksheet — the model organizes evidence; a deterministic rules engine or the caseworker applies the rules.

**Full rules engine, no model.** Rejected as scope: deterministic rules already exist for the clear cases; the bottleneck is reading unstructured applications into structured facts — which is the model's job.

## The first decision

Draw the boundary at *extraction versus determination*. The model handles unstructured input → structured facts with provenance (every extracted fact links to the source document span). Determination — which rules apply and what they yield — happens in inspectable code or human judgment, never in the model.

## Guided reasoning

1. Schema first: define the extraction output — each fact typed, sourced to a document span, nullable when absent.
2. Rule linkage: the model may *suggest* applicable rules with citations to rule text, but cannot *apply* them.
3. Deterministic validation: every extracted fact checked for type, range, and consistency; conflicts surfaced, not resolved silently.
4. The caseworker worksheet shows the model's extraction beside the source — review takes seconds, verification is possible.
5. Denial-path review: any determination that results in denial gets a mandatory human explanation written in the appeal-ready format.
6. Audit: every run records extraction, validations, caseworker edits, and final determination — reconstructable per case.

The [output validation lesson](/learn/genai-app-dev/output-validation-and-moderation) and [audit logs and accountability](/learn/llm-security/audit-logs-and-accountability) carry the mechanics.

## What went wrong

The pilot caught a subtle failure: the model extracted income figures accurately but normalized currency inconsistently — stipends listed monthly in the document became annual figures in extraction, quietly crossing an eligibility threshold. Validation caught the inconsistency only because a unit field was required per figure; the caseworker who'd have caught it by eyeball had been taught to trust the worksheet. Fix: units are extracted, never normalized — the worksheet shows the figure *as stated* with the unit, and any conversion the rules engine performs is shown as a separate, labeled step.

## Failure injection

- Feed an application with deliberately contradictory income documents and check whether the conflict surfaces or resolves silently.
- Submit an application missing a required document and verify the extraction says "absent" rather than inferring a value.
- Replay a denied application end-to-end from logs and confirm the explanation reproduces exactly what the caseworker saw.
- Inject a rule-text change and verify rule suggestions cite the new version, not memorized old text.

## Variants

- **Startup:** even unregulated, the pattern is worth copying — confidence display, escalation path, and a logged human override cost little and age well.
- **Enterprise:** the model sits inside a decision process with existing audit trails — the integration questions (case management, records systems, override workflows) dominate the model questions.
- **Regulated:** the core design constraint — the model advises, a named human decides, and the record must show the human could and did override. "The model recommended it" is not a defense; the documentation of independent judgment is.
- **Low-connectivity:** decision support that needs a live model call fails exactly where decisions can't wait — cached guidance and clear "system unavailable" states are part of the design, not edge cases.

## Two ways to run this

**Whiteboard mode** — read only the situation and constraints, then stop. Sketch your options, decision, and failure plan before opening the rest; the scenario's choices become a grading key for your reasoning, not just a story.

**Guided mode** — read straight through. The options and guided-reasoning sections walk the decision step by step; use them to compare your instinct with the documented one.

## Evaluation rubric

A strong answer keeps a named human as the decision-maker of record, designs the override path so disagreement is easy and recorded, and shows uncertainty as decision input rather than decoration. Weak answers let the model's confidence substitute for the human's judgment, bury the override, or audit-trail only the decisions the system agreed with.
