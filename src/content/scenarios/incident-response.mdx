## The situation

A support assistant answers questions and drafts replies for agent review. On Tuesday, a customer posts a screenshot: the bot promised a full refund the company's policy doesn't allow. Three more screenshots follow within hours. Nothing was deployed this week.

## Constraints

- The feature drafts replies; a human approves sends — but the promised refunds went out anyway, meaning the approval step either failed or was bypassed.
- Customer support is already honoring some promises made in messages; rolling back tone-deaf replies to customers who were promised refunds is a second incident, not a fix.
- The model provider shipped a model update last week; the prompt hasn't changed in a month.
- Leadership wants a public answer "today"; engineering wants to actually know what happened.

## What you don't know

- Whether the approval step failed technically, or the approvers started rubber-stamping — the screenshots can't tell you which.
- Whether the refund language came from the model update, a retrieved document (an old draft with refund language in context), or a genuinely new model behavior.
- How many messages carried the promise before the first screenshot — the blast radius is unknown.
- Whether the eval suite ever tested "does not offer refunds" at all.

## Options on the table

**Roll back the model immediately.** Reasonable containment, but it answers nothing — if the cause was a poisoned retrieved document, rollback changes nothing and you've lost the ability to reproduce.

**Kill the feature.** Containment maximal; information zero. Held in reserve if the blast radius turns out to be large.

**Quarantine to draft-only while investigating.** Chosen: stop all auto-send paths, force every message through human review with explicit refund-language flagging, and replay the last week of drafts through the pipeline to find where the promise entered.

## The first decision

Contain first, diagnose second, communicate third — in that order. The quarantine keeps the feature alive at degraded speed (humans review everything) while forensic replay isolates the cause: same inputs, replayed against the old and new model versions, plus a check of what retrieval returned in the bad cases.

## Guided reasoning

1. Stop the bleeding: force draft-only mode, add a deterministic refund-language flag to the review queue.
2. Preserve evidence: freeze the prompt version, model version, retrieval index, and the failing examples.
3. Reproduce: replay logged inputs through old and new model versions; diff where "refund" enters the text.
4. Find the approval gap: audit the send path — did sends happen without approval events, or did approvers wave them through?
5. Fix the actual cause — retrieval hygiene, a prompt clause, an approval-path bug — not the convenient one.
6. Communicate: what happened, who's affected, what you're honoring, what changed so it doesn't recur.

## What went wrong

The replay isolated it within a day: the provider's model update had shifted instruction-following such that a retrieval result — a deprecated help-center article about an old refund policy — was being adopted as current policy instead of treated as stale context. The approval gap was social: approvers had been waving through drafts at ~4 seconds each; the refund language was plausible enough to pass a skim. Both fixes landed: retrieval freshness filtering, an explicit "never offer refunds" output rule with a deterministic post-check, and an approval UI that highlights policy-sensitive terms instead of relying on reviewer attention.

## Failure injection

- Replay the incident inputs against the next model update before enabling it — make drift detection a release gate.
- Plant a stale refund-policy document in retrieval and confirm the output rule blocks it end to end.
- Simulate an approval event loss mid-send and verify nothing sends on a missing event.
- Measure approver dwell time; if it drops below a threshold, escalate the queue, not the alerts.

## Variants

- **Startup:** the runbook is a paragraph and the on-call is whoever is awake — the quarantine path still needs to exist before the incident, because designing containment mid-incident is how the bad version stays live.
- **Enterprise:** incident comms have a legal review step — the timeline is slower, the wording is negotiated, and the engineering team needs a parallel channel that isn't blocked on messaging approval.
- **Regulated:** notification duties have clocks — the incident response must know which exposures trigger which disclosure windows, and the evidence freeze happens before cleanup.
- **Low-connectivity:** "replay the last week of drafts" assumes you kept them — data-retention choices made for privacy can collide with forensic needs, so the design picks the retention window deliberately.

## Evaluation rubric

A strong answer contains before diagnosing, freezes versions and evidence before fixing, and treats silent-wrongness incidents (plausible bad output) differently from outages. Weak answers roll back first and investigate never, communicate before they know the blast radius, or fix the convenient cause while the real one ships again next release.
