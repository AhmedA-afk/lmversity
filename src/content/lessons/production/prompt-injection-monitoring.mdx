Your guardrail layer will let something through eventually. Monitoring is what tells you it happened before a user does.

## Why the guardrail isn't the whole plan

An input filter or system-prompt defense is a point-in-time check: it looks at one request and decides pass or block. But injection techniques evolve, attackers iterate against your specific defenses, and legitimate content (a pasted email, a scraped webpage, a tool result) can carry an instruction-shaped payload your filter never anticipated. Treating the guardrail as sufficient means your first signal of a new attack pattern is a support ticket or a screenshot on social media, days after it started working.

Monitoring closes that gap by watching what actually happened across many requests, not just judging one request in isolation. It's the difference between a lock on the door and a camera that shows you someone kept jiggling the handle at 3 a.m.

## What to watch for

Build detection around a few concrete signal types, layered on top of your normal [structured logs](/learn/production/structured-logging-for-llm-calls):

- **Injection markers in input.** Phrases like "ignore previous instructions," "you are now," "new system prompt," or attempts to close out delimiters you use internally (`</system>`, triple backticks mid-sentence). None of these are proof on their own, but a spike in their frequency is a real signal — especially in fields that shouldn't contain instructions at all, like a "name" or "notes" input.
- **Behavioral anomalies in output.** A model that suddenly starts responding in an unusual format, refuses a normally-safe request, reveals system-prompt content, or calls a tool it's never called for that user before. This catches injections that don't match any known text pattern because the attack worked and the *behavior* changed.
- **Abuse-shaped usage.** One account issuing a much higher request rate than its history, unusually long or repetitive prompts, or the same payload tried across many accounts in a short window — a signature of someone probing your defenses systematically rather than using the product.
- **Refusal and error-rate shifts.** A jump in your model's own refusal rate or malformed-output rate is often the earliest evidence of adversarial input, showing up before any keyword filter fires.

## Wiring detection into the pipeline

None of this needs a bespoke ML system to start. A practical setup:

<Steps>
<Step title="Score every request">
Run a lightweight classifier or regex/keyword pass over input and output inside the same step where you already do [structured logging](/learn/production/structured-logging-for-llm-calls) — tag each request with a risk score, not a binary block decision.
</Step>
<Step title="Alert on aggregates">
Emit the score as a field and alert on rates — high-risk requests per hour, per user, per endpoint — instead of hand-reviewing every flagged row.
</Step>
<Step title="Route and feed back">
Send anything above threshold to a review queue, and feed confirmed attacks into your guardrail rules and [regression eval suite](/learn/production/eval-based-regression-testing) so the same pattern gets caught earlier next time.
</Step>
<Step title="Throttle the pattern">
Treat repeat offenders as a [rate-limiting](/learn/production/rate-limiting-llm-apps) problem, not just a content-filtering one — someone iterating against your defenses is a traffic pattern you can throttle.
</Step>
</Steps>

The goal isn't a system that blocks everything up front — that's the guardrail's job, and it will never be perfect. It's a system that shortens the time between "an attack started working" and "you know about it," so a novel jailbreak becomes a Tuesday-afternoon fix instead of a week of quiet exploitation.
