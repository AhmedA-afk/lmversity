A model can sit at 100% training accuracy and near-zero validation accuracy for tens of thousands of steps, looking completely overfit — and then, with no change to the recipe, generalize almost overnight. A different model can get *worse* as you add capacity, then get better again past the point where classical statistics says it should have failed permanently. Neither is a glitch. Both are windows into how these networks actually settle on a solution.

This page goes one level further: the mechanism each one is believed to run on, and what that mechanism implies about how gradient descent finds solutions in an overparameterized network.

## Grokking: two circuits competing for the same weights

The clean version of the puzzle: train a small transformer on modular arithmetic (`a + b mod p`), and training accuracy hits 100% almost immediately — trivial to memorize a fixed table of input-output pairs. Validation accuracy stays near chance for a long stretch afterward. Then, abruptly, it jumps to near-100% too.

The mechanistic account that's emerged from studying these toy settings: gradient descent doesn't search for "the generalizing solution" directly — it finds whatever solution is easiest to reach first, and a lookup-table memorization is enormously easier to reach than a structured algorithm, because it requires no shared structure across examples at all. Once the memorized solution has driven training loss to zero, the *training* gradient signal goes essentially quiet, but two things are still active: weight decay is still pulling every weight toward zero, and there's typically a small amount of remaining gradient pressure from the softmax not yet being fully saturated. Under continued weight decay, the memorizing solution — which needs one arbitrary, high-norm weight pattern per training example — is more expensive to maintain than a generalizing circuit, which needs to encode only the underlying structure (in the modular-arithmetic case, something closer to a Fourier-basis representation of addition) once. As training continues, the generalizing circuit's representation is quietly growing inside the same weights, and once its output starts to *out-compete* the memorized lookup table on the token-prediction logits, validation accuracy flips from chance to correct in a comparatively small number of steps — a phase transition, not a gradual climb.

That account explains the two facts that otherwise look contradictory: memorization comes first because it's the path of least resistance for gradient descent on a fresh network, and generalization comes late because it's cheaper *in weight norm*, not cheaper to discover. Turn off weight decay in these experiments and grokking typically stops appearing — the model stays memorized indefinitely, because nothing is pushing the more expensive lookup-table solution to lose its lead.

## Double descent: why more capacity can un-hurt you

Classical statistics predicts a U-shaped test-error curve: error falls as a model gets more expressive, bottoms out, then rises as the model starts fitting noise — the textbook picture of overfitting. Double descent is what you see if you don't stop increasing capacity at that bottom. Right around the **interpolation threshold** — the point where the model has just enough capacity to fit the training data exactly, with no slack — test error spikes, exactly as the classical story predicts. Push capacity *past* that threshold, and test error falls again, often below the first minimum.

The mechanism centers on what "just enough capacity" does to the solution space. At the interpolation threshold, there's typically exactly one way (or very few) to fit the training data perfectly, and that one fit is forced to contort itself through every training point with no room to spare — small changes in which examples you trained on produce wildly different fits. That's high variance in the classical bias-variance sense, and it's why error spikes there. Past the threshold, there are *many* ways to fit the training data — the model is underdetermined by the data — and gradient descent, especially with weight decay or an implicit bias toward small-norm solutions, tends to land on one of the smoother, simpler fits among that much larger set. More capacity doesn't just mean "more ways to overfit" — it means more ways to fit the data at all, and some of those extra ways generalize better than the single forced fit at the threshold did.

The same shape shows up along three different axes, and the mechanism is the same one each time: **model-wise** (more parameters at fixed data and training length), **epoch-wise** (training longer at fixed model size — a large model can look like it's overfitting mid-training, at exactly the point where it's memorized the training set but hasn't yet found a flatter fit, then improve again with more steps), and **sample-wise** (more data at a fixed, large model size, where *adding* data can transiently increase test error near the point where the added data first pushes the model to the interpolation threshold).

## The shared thread: memorization isn't the end state, it's a waypoint

Both phenomena point at the same underlying claim about how overparameterized networks learn: the training loss curve hitting zero (or near it) is not evidence that the model has finished finding its solution. A network with headroom left — extra weight-decay pressure still applying, extra capacity still available past the interpolation threshold — keeps moving through the space of solutions that all fit the training data equally well, and that movement can trade memorization for generalization well after the loss curve looks flat.

Grokking is this transition viewed along the *training-time* axis at fixed capacity; double descent is essentially the same "more room to find a better fit" story viewed along the *capacity* axis. Neither would make sense under the assumption that gradient descent stops improving once training loss bottoms out — both are evidence that it keeps reshaping the solution using degrees of freedom the loss function alone can't see.

## What this suggests about how LLMs learn

Pull back from the toy settings and the implication for the [pretraining](/learn/llm-foundations/pretraining-explained) picture is this: a smoothly falling [scaling-law loss curve](/learn/llm-foundations/scaling-laws-what-they-predict) is an average over an enormous number of individual sub-tasks, each of which may be undergoing its own local version of a memorize-then-generalize transition, at different points in training and at different scales. The aggregate curve looks continuous because it's summing over thousands of these transitions happening at staggered points — which is a mechanistically different, and more specific, story than "the model gradually gets a little better at everything at once." It also reframes what "overparameterized" means in practice: a model with far more parameters than the training set strictly requires isn't automatically primed to overfit — double descent is direct evidence that going past the danger zone can be safer than stopping inside it.

<Callout kind="breaks" title="Where the analogy breaks down">
Grokking and double descent were both demonstrated cleanly on small, often synthetic tasks (algorithmic datasets, small vision benchmarks) — not measured as a checkpoint-by-checkpoint phenomenon on a production LLM pretraining run, where compute budgets rarely allow training a fixed model for orders of magnitude longer than needed just to watch a transition happen. Treat both as *mechanisms that are real and well-demonstrated in controlled settings*, not as a guarantee that any specific stalled validation curve you're staring at right now is secretly mid-grok. The correct response to a flat validation curve is still to check it against held-out data properly, not to assume a phase transition is imminent.
</Callout>
