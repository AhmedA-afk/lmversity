For all their apparent variety, large language models are trained on a single, almost absurdly simple objective: given a sequence of tokens, predict the next one. Everything else — answering questions, writing code, holding a conversation — falls out of doing that one thing extremely well.

## How the objective works

During training the model reads enormous amounts of text. At every position it makes a prediction — a probability for each token in the vocabulary of what comes next — and is corrected toward the token that actually followed. Repeat across trillions of tokens and the model is pushed to internalise grammar, facts, style, and reasoning patterns, because all of them help it guess the next token more accurately.

The output is not a single word; it's a **distribution** over every possible next token, turned into probabilities by [the softmax function](/learn/maths-foundations/the-softmax-function).

## Generation is prediction in a loop

At inference time the same machinery generates text:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Feed in the prompt">The prompt is supplied to the model as tokens.</Step>
  <Step title="Predict a distribution">The model predicts a distribution over the next token.</Step>
  <Step title="Sample one token">One token is sampled from that distribution.</Step>
  <Step title="Append it to the sequence">The sampled token is appended to the running sequence.</Step>
  <Step title="Go back to step 2">The loop repeats, predicting a new distribution over what follows.</Step>
</Steps>

That loop is the whole of "the model is typing." A chat reply is just this running until the model predicts a stop token.

## Why one objective is enough

It feels too simple to explain reasoning — but predicting the next token in *hard* text (a proof, a working function, a coherent argument) requires actually modelling the structure underneath it. Push that far enough, at scale, and useful capabilities emerge.
