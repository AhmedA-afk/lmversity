This course matters the moment a brief contains the words "AI-powered" and nobody can say what the feature actually does when it is unsure, wrong, slow, or blocked. It teaches you to answer that — first as a judgment call, then as behavior, then as interface, then as evidence.

## The two lanes

Every lesson in this course serves one of two jobs, and the capstone joins them:

1. **Using AI inside your design work** — research synthesis, ideation, critique, content drafting — without letting the tool make the judgment. That lane expands in later modules; the MVP keeps it light so you can focus.
2. **Designing product experiences that contain AI** — behavior, states, uncertainty, recovery, permissions, evaluation, handoff. This is the MVP's spine, and it is what your case study demonstrates.

You do not need coding experience for the core path. Optional labs may invite you to inspect light HTML or JSON, or to use a no-code tool, and each one says so up front. Nothing in the MVP requires buying a particular tool.

## What you will make

Each module ends in a reusable artifact, and the capstone assembles them:

- Module 1: a one-page **AI opportunity brief** — user problem, proposed role for AI, non-AI baseline, value hypothesis, failure costs, decision owner.
- Module 2: a **behavior contract and state diagram** for your chosen feature.
- Module 3: an annotated flow showing **uncertainty, evidence, correction, undo, escalation, and approval**.
- Module 4: a **prototype, scenario set, evaluation rubric, findings log, and revision note**.
- Capstone: the full case study plus an **implementation-ready interaction spec** an engineer can review without guessing.

If you are a visual, brand, research, or content designer who has never designed software, you are a first-class reader here, not an edge case. Where a lesson needs software-product context — state models, handoff — it introduces that context instead of presuming it. If you are an experienced product designer, use the `-compared` and `-common-mistakes` pages as a diagnostic skim, then move fast to Module 4's evaluation method.

## Prerequisites

You need working design fundamentals in your own discipline and nothing else. Two short pieces of background will make Module 1 much easier, and both are free in this site's curriculum:

- [How LLMs work](/learn/ai-foundations/how-llms-work) — what the model is actually doing when it generates text.
- [A mental model for what LLMs can and can't do](/learn/ai-foundations/what-llms-can-and-cannot-do) — where the boundaries sit.

## What this course does not teach

It is not a model-engineering course, not a tool review site, and not a list of prompt tricks. Model internals live in the existing tracks; vendor interfaces change too fast to be the core. What stays durable — design reasoning, failure states, evaluation, accessibility, handoff — is what this course owns.

## How to work through the MVP

Read Modules 1 through 4 in order; each one assumes the artifact of the previous one. Pick your capstone feature early — three bounded briefs are offered in the [capstone](/learn/ai-for-designers/capstone-design-a-trustworthy-ai-feature), or define your own with the scope check there — and carry the same feature through every module artifact. That continuity is what turns four exercises into one portfolio case study.

**Related:** [A designer's mental model for generative AI](/learn/ai-for-designers/designer-mental-model-for-generative-ai), [When a workflow beats an agent](/learn/agentic-ai/when-not-to-use-an-agent)

## Your first step

Before the next lesson, write down the feature you will carry through the course in two sentences: who it serves and what job the AI does. You will test that choice against evidence in the [worked example](/learn/ai-for-designers/choosing-where-ai-belongs-worked-example). If you cannot name the non-AI baseline yet, leave it blank — Module 1 will force the comparison.
