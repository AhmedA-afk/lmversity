These are the mistakes people make in their first year of using AI tools — not because they're careless, but because fluent output *feels* verified. Each entry names the mistake, why it's tempting, and the habit that replaces it.

## 1. Treating fluency as accuracy

**Wrong:** A confident, well-written answer reads as a correct answer, so it gets forwarded unchecked.
**Right:** Fluency measures how text reads, not whether it's true. Verify any claim you'd be embarrassed to be wrong about — names, dates, numbers, quotes, legal and medical statements. The model's job is plausibility, not truth.

## 2. Asking for sources, then not checking them

**Wrong:** "Give me a citation" produces a citation-shaped object — real authors, plausible journal, wrong title — and it gets pasted into the document.
**Right:** Treat every generated citation as a lead, not a reference. Open the link or search the title before citing it.

## 3. Pasting private data into shared tools

**Wrong:** Client names, salaries, unpublished code, or a colleague's health detail go into the prompt because that's the fastest way to get help with them.
**Right:** Assume anything typed into a consumer tool may be retained or reviewed. Redact names and figures, or use an approved enterprise tool with a data agreement. Check your organization's policy before the first paste, not after.

## 4. Asking the model to decide instead of draft

**Wrong:** "Should I fire this person / take this loan / end this contract" — the model answers with confidence, and the answer gets treated as a recommendation from something that understood the situation.
**Right:** The model doesn't know your context and can't weigh what it doesn't know. Use it to enumerate options and surface considerations; the decision — and its consequences — stays with you.

## 5. Copy-pasting output without reading for your context

**Wrong:** Generated email/code/essay gets sent as-is — including the hallucinated detail, the wrong name, the subtly-off tone.
**Right:** The model wrote a draft. Read it as if a stranger wrote it: check every name, every number, every claim, and the parts it made up to fill gaps you didn't notice it filling.

## 6. Believing the model knows what it doesn't know

**Wrong:** Asking "are you sure?" and trusting the confident "yes" — or trusting a stated confidence level.
**Right:** Models don't carry calibrated self-knowledge. "Are you sure" invites a performance of confidence, not a measurement of it. Verify against the source, not against the model's own assurance.

## 7. Using the chat box for a task with a real tool

**Wrong:** Asking the model to "add these 40 numbers," "check this spreadsheet," "translate this legal clause" — when a calculator, a spreadsheet, or a professional does the job reliably.
**Right:** Models are strong at drafting, summarizing, and reframing; weak at arithmetic, precise extraction, and anything where the error is invisible. If a deterministic tool exists, use it — or verify the model's arithmetic yourself.

## 8. Assuming the free tier equals the enterprise product

**Wrong:** Learning the tool on a personal account, then using it at work the same way — same prompts, same data, no policy check.
**Right:** Consumer and enterprise tiers differ on data retention, review, and training use. The interface looks identical; the data handling isn't. Verify which product you're actually using before putting work data in it.

## If you take one habit

Verify the things that would embarrass you. The model's fluency is real and its errors are invisible — the only reliable detector is a human checking the specific claims that matter.
